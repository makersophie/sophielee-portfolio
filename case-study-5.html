A dementia institution search platform — built from two rounds of research and one critical reframe.

Booking Care — platform homepage and search interface

Helping families find the right dementia care institution — by surfacing how institutions actually care, not just that they exist.

Background

A problem every family faces, but no one had solved properly

In Taiwan, finding the right dementia care institution is an overwhelming process. Information is scattered across government registries, outdated websites, and word of mouth. Families in crisis — already exhausted from caregiving — have no reliable way to compare institutions, understand what care they can provide, or know if they'll accept their family member at all.

Our team chose this problem because it was personal. All of us had watched a family member go through the search process. We knew the fatigue firsthand. What we didn't know yet was exactly what we should build — and that required us to go out and find out.

Early team workshop — mapping the problem space on Miro
Research

What caregivers actually said when we asked

We ran two rounds of interviews — one with family caregivers, one with a lead operating a dementia institution. What we heard challenged the assumptions we'd walked in with.

From caregivers:

Caregiver

"The information on the internet is too messy to find. I cannot find a dementia institution that can fulfill my need."

Caregiver

"I was rejected by many institutions. They said my mother-in-law's situation is too complicated."

Caregiver

"I'm stressed out, but nobody understands my tiredness. I have no one to tell how hard it is to take care of a dementia patient day and night."

From the institution expert:

Expert Interview — Institution Lead

"Most of the time we accept requests. But when a patient has night wandering, or is in late-stage dementia, or needs three tubes — our capacity isn't enough even if we want to accept. We've seen so many primary family caregivers, mostly female, completely burn out. They can't stop, and it harms them physically and mentally."

The research painted a clear picture of two parallel frustrations: caregivers couldn't find the right institution, and institutions couldn't efficiently communicate what they could and couldn't accept.

Reframe

We almost built the wrong thing

Our first read of the data pointed strongly toward the emotional pain. Caregivers were exhausted, isolated, and overwhelmed. Our first HMW statement reflected that:

HMW design a product that helps dementia caregivers release their negative feelings?

We ran with it. We brainstormed, ideated, and built a concept around emotional support and stress relief tools. Then we tested it.

The feedback was clarifying in a way we hadn't expected:

"Although you gave us ways to release stress — we still can't feel confident. We still have to go home and take care of them afterwards."

— Concept testing participant

Releasing stress wasn't the real need. It was a symptom. The underlying need was confidence — the feeling that their family member was being well looked after, so they could finally stop worrying.

The Reframe
HMW v1 (wrong)

How might we help dementia caregivers release their negative feelings?

HMW v2 (right)

How might we create an experience where caregivers feel confident that their family member is being well cared for?

This single reframe changed everything. An emotional support tool wouldn't solve it. What caregivers needed was a reliable way to find, evaluate, and connect with the right institution — one that matched their family member's specific situation and gave them visible proof of how care was delivered.

Design

From competitive gap to product concept

Before designing, I mapped every existing dementia care search platform in Taiwan — focusing on information architecture, filtering capabilities, and how institutions were represented. The gap was consistent across all of them: none offered matching based on specific care needs, and none showed caregivers what care actually looked like inside the institution.

Competitive analysis — 4 existing Taiwan platforms (長照地圖, U主長照, ALTC, 台北市聯合醫院)

The MVP had one job: get caregivers to the right institution faster, with enough information to feel confident before they ever picked up the phone. Three core features:

👩
User Goal

"I need to find an institution that can actually accept my family member, and I need to be able to trust that they'll be taken care of properly."

① Filter for dementia needs

Most platforms offered only location and price filters. We built a filtering system around the variables that actually determine eligibility: dementia severity (mild / moderate / severe), patient mobility, and eventually the three medical tubes (三管) — nasogastric tube, urinary catheter, and tracheostomy — which many institutions cannot support.

② Institution listings with real care evidence

Search results showed institutions on a map alongside a list view with pricing, availability, and — critically — photos and videos of actual care environments. Not stock imagery. Real spaces, real routines. This was what made caregivers feel something they'd been missing: a sense of what life would actually look like for their family member there.

③ Online reservation form

The path from "interested" to "scheduled a visit" required multiple phone calls on every existing platform. We simplified it to a single online form — submitting care needs, patient details, and preferred visit times in one step.

Testing & Iteration

What two weeks of testing taught us

We tested the MVP with 2 caregivers and 1 institution lead. Both sides confirmed the core concept, but each gave us something specific to act on.

Caregiver Feedback

The filtering was useful, but what they wanted most wasn't just to find an institution — it was to understand how it would care for their family member. They wanted more visibility into the day-to-day.

Institution Feedback

The institution lead said it was the most complete information architecture they'd seen in any Taiwan platform. Their ask: make it even easier for institutions to show caregivers what they do.

Three changes shaped the final design from here:

  • 1
    Added 三管 (three tubes) to the filter A second round of interviews revealed that for many families, whether an institution could handle nasogastric tubes, urinary catheters, or tracheostomy tubes was the single most decisive factor. We rebuilt the filter system to surface this upfront.
  • 2
    Replaced staged photos with real care documentation Institutions don't have marketing budgets. Most couldn't produce polished photography. We redesigned the media section around authentic documentation — real staff interactions, real routines — which turned out to be more trustworthy to caregivers anyway.
  • 3
    Added a guided matching test for first-timers For caregivers who didn't yet know the terminology — what stage is "moderate dementia"? what does 三管 mean? — we added a short diagnostic quiz that translated their situation into a recommended filter set, so they could start searching with confidence even without prior knowledge.
Final design — updated filter system including 三管 criteria
Matching test — guided onboarding for first-time caregivers
Reflection

What this project taught me about asking the right question

  • 1
    The right problem is not always the loudest one. Emotional pain was the most visible thing in our research. But the real unmet need was confidence — the sense that the situation was under control. Getting to that required concept testing, not just more interviews. Artifacts that fail tell you things that questions don't.
  • 2
    Reframing a HMW statement is a design decision. Changing from "release negative feelings" to "create confidence in care quality" wasn't just a wording change — it completely redirected the product. The willingness to throw out weeks of ideation because the framing was wrong is something I've carried into every project since.
  • 3
    Both sides of a two-sided platform have to win. Designing for caregivers without designing for institutions would have created a platform institutions didn't participate in. The expert interview gave us the institutional perspective early enough to design around it, not accommodate it as an afterthought.
  • 4
    Authentic evidence beats polished presentation. Real care photos — imperfect, unstaged — built more trust with caregivers than anything else we tested. In high-stakes decisions, authenticity carries more weight than production value. That's a principle that extends well beyond this project.
